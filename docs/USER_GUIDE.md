## 1. Before anything else

Three things decide how everything on this site should be read.

**The numbers are relative, not absolute.** Every monitoring site is compared
against its own history, as a z-score. Zero means normal for that site. Above
one means high for that site. A county at High is not necessarily worse off
than a county at Typical somewhere else; it has moved further from its own
normal. Comparing two places on raw concentration would be misleading, because
one plant may serve 5,000 people and another 500,000, and laboratories differ.

**They are not case counts.** A z-score of 2.0 does not convert into a number
of infected people. Shedding varies enormously between individuals and over the
course of an illness.

**A county with no data is not a county with no transmission.** Coverage is
partial and uneven. The map marks counties with no monitored site separately
from counties that are quiet, and the two mean entirely different things.

As an example, in the run described here SARS-CoV-2 sat at a median site z-score of -0.11 across 1,875 sites in 1,089 counties.

## 3. Reading an activity level

| Level | Median site z-score | Plain reading |
|---|---|---|
| Very Low | z at or below -1 | Well below normal for this place. |
| Low | -1 to -0.25 | Below its usual range. |
| Typical | -0.25 to 0.25 | About normal for this place. |
| Elevated | 0.25 to 1 | Above its usual range. |
| High | above 1 | Well above normal for this place. |

The level shown for a county is the median across its reporting sites. The
national level is the median across all reporting sites.

## 4. Setting up an email alert

1. Open **My alerts** and enter your email address. You will be sent a sign-in
   link; there is no password to choose or remember.
2. Choose a pathogen, a state and the level you care about.
3. Save the rule. Each weekly run is matched against your saved rules, and you
   are emailed when one matches.

A rule is yours alone. Row-level security in the database restricts every saved
rule to the account that created it.

## 5. Questions people ask

**Why is the date at the top not today?**
It is the latest *sample* date, not today's date. Samples take days to reach a
laboratory, be tested, and be published. The header also shows when CDC last
refreshed the data.

**Why did a number change since last week?**
CDC revises its datasets continuously. Every page is built from one frozen
weekly snapshot, and each snapshot is archived. If you cite a figure, cite the
run identifier and the data-through date shown in the header, so the exact
numbers can be recovered later.

**What does an empty panel mean?**
That a source did not load, not that the measurement came back empty. Empty
panels say which source failed and why, and the Audit log records the outcome
of every source for the run.

**Does wastewater give early warning?**
Sometimes, and less than is usually claimed. The Lead time page splits warning
time into the part that is biology and the part that is reporting delay. A lead
of two weeks is worth nothing if publication also takes two weeks.

**Can any of this identify me?**
No. Everything here comes from CDC's published, aggregated data at
treatment-plant and county level. No sample is tied to a person, a household or
an address, and no sub-building sampling is used or reported.

## 6. Citing the site

Cite the release for the run, not the live page, because the live page moves
every week. Each run is archived as a permanent release containing its tables,
figures and export. The About page carries the full citation format, and the
run identifier in the header tells you which release to cite.
